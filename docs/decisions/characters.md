## Frankie “Chips” Caruso

- **Identity:** 47, Italian-American, raised in Newark; former casino enforcer.
- **Reason for coming:** collect unpaid money from Voss and settle what he calls his last piece of business here.
- **Inner conflict:** thinks loyalty excuses what he once did. His arc is accepting responsibility rather than protecting Voss's reputation.
- **Working personal connection:** he escorted a marked guest to the cashier cage and obeyed an instruction to leave. He has never asked what happened afterward. The guest is Martin Keene; Frankie escorted him to the cage in 1973. Keene’s fate remains open. See the [Frankie dossier](character-backstories/frankie.md).
- **Voice:** worn middle-low register; restrained North Jersey accent; clipped consonants and economical sentences. Irritation arrives quickly; tenderness arrives reluctantly. Avoid a cartoon mobster impression.
- **Humor:** practical complaints, grudges, and recognition of former guests.
- **Appearance:** broad, heavy silhouette; tobacco tailoring, loosened collar, worn formal shoes. Old staff identification remains in his wallet.
- **Audition line:** “I know that knock. Don't open it. ... Yeah. I used to be on the other side.”

### Character design for review

Frankie knows everyone's name and remembers the night he hurt them. He claims his concern is getting paid; in practice, he habitually puts himself between danger and whoever looks least prepared. His tenderness is expressed as irritation, advice, and checking whether someone has eaten. He straightens an overturned chair before realizing how absurd that is during an outbreak.

Short dark hair thinning at the crown, a broken nose, a heavy jaw, and a tired face give him a recognizable profile without intricate facial animation. His tobacco jacket is good clothing worn too long, with a cream open-collar shirt and a gold watch that no longer runs. Broad shoulders and a slightly forward stance distinguish him from Voss's upright formality.

His weakness is confusing obedience with honor. He is dangerous, capable of kindness, and implicated in what happened here. Do not excuse his violence by making him merely a lovable uncle. His humor draws on petty professional grievances and treating the undead as former customers who are still causing trouble; it is not a stream of Italian-food references or mob-film quotations.

## Evelyn “Eve” Vale

- **Identity:** 34, Black British, raised in south London; touring illusionist and casino headliner.
- **Reason for coming:** retrieve her missing stage partner's effects from Voss's custody.
- **Inner conflict:** treating danger as a trick gives her control until she must admit there are stakes she cannot misdirect away.
- **Working personal connection:** her stage partner and friend Arthur “Art” Bell noticed that an apparent stage passage did not match the building plan and disappeared after investigating. His exact fate remains open. Eve had allowed him to lose public credit before their final argument; see the [Eve dossier](character-backstories/eve.md).
- **Voice:** low smoky contralto; educated London English with a softer south London edge in private speech. Precise setups, deliberate pauses, dry final words. Fear makes her unusually direct, not more theatrical.
- **Humor:** exposing pretension and bad misdirection.
- **Appearance:** tailored stage trousers; asymmetrical ivory-and-black jacket with oxblood lining; repaired cuffs and concealed practical tools. Avoid a costume consisting only of a top hat and cape.
- **Audition line:** “A false wall, a borrowed key, an audience looking the wrong way. That part I understand.”

### Character design for review

Eve can command a room but dislikes being personally known. Her stage confidence is learned craft. She automatically studies sightlines, exits, and what someone wants her to notice. A flaw in a performance annoys her even when she should be afraid. Away from an audience, she carefully repairs her own equipment and keeps her missing partner's place in the act intact.

Angular face, a strong brow, and short natural curls create a clear profile. The ivory jacket has one black lapel and a dark oxblood lining visible in motion. Practical high-waisted black trousers and low boots make the outfit usable in combat. Her hands are bare for sleight of hand; a narrow dark wrist wrap becomes a first-person identifier.

She initially treats explanations as contests she must win. Her emotional change is allowing someone else to see that she does not know the answer. Her comedy exposes poor craftsmanship, vanity, and obvious misdirection. Do not make every line a magic pun or let supernatural knowledge make her infallible.

## Leon Mercer

- **Identity:** 39, Black American, raised in southeast Texas; casino maintenance engineer.
- **Reason for coming:** complete the shutdown and recover his maintenance records before management removes them.
- **Inner conflict:** pride in having kept the building running collides with what that work enabled.
- **Working personal connection:** installed a separately metered circuit for the secure cash area under an off-book work order. He did not know its purpose. A prior industrial accident explains why accepting that assurance matters to him. His wife Della and daughter Ruthie are alive and waiting for him; see the [Leon dossier](character-backstories/leon.md). This is infrastructure serving the bargain, not its scientific explanation.
- **Voice:** warm middle register, lightly textured; measured southeast Texas vowels without exaggerated drawl. Usually finishes a thought before speaking. Under pressure his sentences become short and exact.
- **Humor:** patient understatement and literal assessments of absurd problems. He is technically capable, not the team's gullible skeptic.
- **Appearance:** faded teal maintenance clothes, rolled sleeves, repaired watch, labeled keys. His clothing looks worked in rather than generically dirty.
- **Audition line:** “That line's disconnected. Has been all week. So whatever's ringing, let it.”

### Character design for review

Leon is patient because he has spent years being called only when something breaks. He notices who gets credit, who leaves a mess, and which repair was deferred to save money. He cares about the staff who use the building, not its owner's prestige. He restores old radios off shift and is quietly particular about music reproduction.

A long face, close-cropped hair, a neat mustache, and lean working build distinguish him from Frankie. Faded teal coveralls are worn open over a sand-colored work shirt, with sleeves rolled evenly. A repaired watch and two labeled keys are more readable than an overloaded tool belt. He pauses to listen to a mechanism before touching it.

He recognizes evidence of the supernatural quickly; what frustrates him is that someone still signed a work order for it. His flaw is assuming he can personally keep every failing system—and every person—going. His humor uses literal descriptions, patient corrections, and controlled anger about negligence. He must also get decisive, forceful moments rather than only reacting to other people's jokes.

## Vivian Cross

- **Identity:** 29, Mexican-American, raised in Los Angeles; professional card cheat and confidence artist. Born Vivian Cruz, professionally Cross.
- **Reason for coming:** rob the vault using an invitation issued to Celia March. Vivian does not yet know at entry that the historical March is dead.
- **Inner conflict:** is excellent at planning her own exit and unpracticed at returning for someone else.
- **Working personal connection:** believed she chose the stolen identity herself. Evidence suggests the invitation was deliberately put within her reach. The invitation bears Celia March’s name; March’s history and the planting mechanism remain open. Vivian wants the score partly to repay her brother Tomas, whose print shop she helped ruin; see the [Vivian dossier](character-backstories/vivian.md).
- **Voice:** bright, lightly husky middle register; Los Angeles American English, quick consonants, playful rising setups followed by abrupt flat conclusions. Negotiation is musical; sincerity loses that performance. Do not infer a Spanish accent from her heritage.
- **Humor:** pricing danger, testing people, puncturing authority. Allow sincere lines without punchlines.
- **Appearance:** narrow plum suit, patterned shirt, neat gloves; expensive-looking garments with altered labels and discreet pockets.
- **Audition line:** “I can open the safe. Getting us out afterward? That's the part where I need you.”

### Character design for review

Vivian likes discovering what people want because it tells her which version of herself to present. Her easy confidence hides continual calculation about money and escape. She is an excellent cheat and a poor winner: the urge to let someone know they were fooled can undo her caution. Her sincere concern becomes audible when the charm abruptly disappears.

A rounded face with a strong nose, dark chin-length hair tucked behind one ear, and an alert, mobile stance separate her from Eve. Her plum suit has a pale patterned shirt, narrow tan gloves, and flat shoes kept unusually clean. A modest imitation-gold bracelet completes the expensive appearance; no impractical dangling accessories are needed.

Eve performs for an audience; Vivian performs differently for each person. Make that distinction audible. Vivian's comedy comes from bargaining, shameless self-interest, and compliments that reveal a con a moment too late. Avoid constant modern quips. Her change is choosing an obligation to the crew that she cannot profit from or explain away.

## Vincent Voss

**Established:** theatrical, intimidating, charming kingpin. An NPC, not a playable crew member, is the working implementation direction.

- **Identity:** approximately 58; American casino owner who has cultivated an elegant public speaking voice. Working origin: Atlantic City, born around 1918. His former wife Helena is alive; she helped design the casino and left him in 1969. See the [Voss dossier](character-backstories/voss.md).
- **Behavior:** treats the casino as his stage and hospitality as a form of ownership. Remembers a favorite drink and the name of someone a guest owes money to. Both are ways of applying pressure.
- **Voice:** resonant baritone; cultivated mid-Atlantic stage diction with an occasional harder American vowel when control slips. Broad, warm public announcements; intimate, quieter threats. Articulation and pauses do more work than pitch or shouting.
- **Humor:** impeccable customer-service language applied to appalling events. He believes he is entitled to be amused; he is not constantly delivering jokes.
- **Threat rule:** the scarier he becomes, the less he performs. Do not make every line a villain monologue or a growl.
- **Appearance:** midnight-blue dinner jacket, carefully groomed silvering hair, distinctive old signet ring. His younger portrait wears the same ring. Avoid default cigar/fedora shorthand.
- **Relationship:** knows Frankie personally; treats Eve as talent he owns, Leon as an employee he can still order, and Vivian as an entertaining threat. His attachment to Marlowe is sincere, though its history is open.
- **Audition, public:** “Ladies and gentlemen, your patience has been exquisite. Unfortunately, patience is not legal tender.”
- **Audition, private:** “Frankie. Leave the key on the counter. I'd hate to ask you as an employee.”

### Character design for review

Voss is genuinely good at making someone feel chosen. He recalls a detail from a conversation years ago, has a waiter bring the right drink, and turns a room's attention toward a guest with apparent generosity. He regards the loyalty this earns as a debt he can collect. The warmth works because some of it is real.

Tall, still, and meticulously upright, with swept-back silvering hair, a long face, heavy-lidded eyes, and a broad practiced smile. Midnight-blue tailoring, a cream shirt, and a restrained black bow tie give him the presence of the person hosting the evening. Keep the old signet ring as his identifying object. A face capable of warmth will eventually matter more than a permanent villain scowl.

When challenged publicly, he enlarges the performance and makes the room laugh with him. When truly threatened, he uses the person's name, lowers his voice, and gives one very specific instruction. His flaw is believing people can only be bought, owned, or intimidated. The crew's freely chosen loyalty is something his scheme fails to account for. His humor is extravagant courtesy used to conceal coercion. Do not use generic snarling, constant shouting, or a joke after every threat.

## Marlowe

- **Identity:** Edwin Marlowe, appears about 63; long-serving bartender originally from near Leeds, working for Voss by 1959. His exact age and supernatural status remain open. His sister Elsie is alive in England. See the [Marlowe dossier](character-backstories/marlowe.md).
- **Voice:** dry, airy middle-low register, light northern English accent. Unhurried, courteous, and plain. Voss fills a room; Marlowe lets a silence do the work.
- **Behavior:** remembers orders and notices absence. His clues sound like observations from a working bartender, not prophetic riddles.
- **Humor:** small corrections delivered without inviting laughter.
- **Appearance:** meticulously kept burgundy staff waistcoat, older badge and casino crest, practical white shirt. Preserve recognition of the existing bartender while improving the model later.
- **Audition line:** “Checked out, yes. Left? That's a different column.”

### Character design for review

Marlowe remains occupied while everyone else performs. He checks a glass, replaces a coaster, or finishes an order before answering. He remembers staff as carefully as wealthy guests. His apparent neutrality is his moral weakness: attending to the bar lets him postpone deciding whether to intervene.

Narrow, slightly stooped silhouette; receding gray hair, prominent ears, and an attentive face. White sleeves, burgundy waistcoat, and a small old-fashioned badge connect him to the existing model. Keep gestures economical so a later decision to stop working and look directly at someone carries weight.

He has witnessed enough to be specific, not omniscient. His comedy corrects a tiny factual detail while leaving the surrounding horror untouched. He should sometimes answer plainly and help practically; endless riddles would make him irritating. His exact debt and supernatural status remain unresolved. There is no current blanket magical ban on telling the truth: fear, complicity, incomplete knowledge and distrust explain his evasions.

## Heightened characterization — current performance direction

Established user correction: characters should be exaggerated, outlandish, dramatic, and occasionally over the top. Their jokes must come from their individual perspectives. This supersedes the earlier emphasis on restrained personalities; grounded rendering and real emotional stakes remain compatible with extravagant behavior.

- **Frankie: the self-appointed guardian of civilized violence.** Has an elaborate personal code for conduct while threatening, fighting, and protecting people. Takes a zombie biting him as a disgraceful breach of etiquette. Addresses the dead as though they are being thrown out of a private function. His temper can expand into wounded, operatic indignation, then collapse into a petty complaint. His loyalty and guilt remain real. Broader lapels and expressive hands support the performance; avoid ethnic caricature.
- **Eve: a furious star confronted with an unauthorized production.** Treats the haunting as a rival act with unlimited funding and appalling taste. May announce an impossible escape before knowing how to accomplish it. Critiques supernatural spectacle, takes its successes personally, and tries to seize the audience's attention back. Let polished diction erupt into grand declarations and indignant asides. Her fear for her partner remains the subject she cannot turn into performance.
- **Leon: a magnificently offended expert.** Treats a building attempting to kill him as gross professional misconduct. Addresses machinery like a stubborn colleague, delivers diagnoses with mounting sermon-like conviction, and threatens to dismantle things that should inspire religious terror. Warm measured speech can build into a precise, thunderous verdict. His fixes work; the comedy is the scale of his offense, not incompetence or accent alone.
- **Vivian: an audacious entrepreneur of catastrophe.** Sees an apocalypse as an unsettled market and herself as its most promising fraud. Invents titles, offers, and escape plans with magnificent certainty, including while being rescued. Confidence sometimes turns into an enormous bluff she must live up to. Fast conspiratorial pitches, mock sincerity, and sudden honest panic distinguish her from Eve's stage declarations. Her affection eventually costs her something she would previously have sold.
- **Voss: the proprietor conducting his own damnation.** Addresses the catastrophe like the most lavish event he has ever hosted. Gives monstrous demands the ceremony of an exclusive invitation. Relishes announcements, grand promises, and luxurious euphemisms. His public excess makes the occasional intimate, quiet threat more frightening. Never soften the fact that he will sacrifice people to maintain control.
- **Marlowe: etiquette maintained past the point of sanity.** His behavior is extravagantly inappropriate even when his voice stays dry. Requires a coaster during a massacre, regards a severed hand chiefly as something abandoned on his counter, and treats supernatural possession as no excuse to run a tab. Give him outrageous priorities, not only cryptic clues. He is the cast's contrast in volume, not a smaller personality.

A character's occupation is the starting point, not their entire joke vocabulary. Frankie judges manners and loyalty; Eve judges taste and attention; Leon judges responsibility and competence; Vivian judges value and leverage; Voss judges ownership and prestige; Marlowe judges order and service. Apply those judgments to combat, money, fear, friendships, and their own failures.

## Ensemble design for review

The four protagonists have different ways of trying to control danger: Frankie protects and intimidates; Eve explains and redirects attention; Leon repairs and organizes; Vivian negotiates and escapes. Their arc is learning when to accept someone else's method.

Voss performs hospitality to keep people obligated; Marlowe provides hospitality while avoiding a choice. The contrast makes their eventual choices more meaningful.

Test individuality by writing reactions to the same situation. For a paid door that will not open, Frankie takes the insult personally, Eve spots the deception, Leon diagnoses deliberate sabotage, Vivian considers charging admission from the other side, Voss calls it an exclusive privilege, and Marlowe remembers who last had the key. These are writing intentions, not finished one-liners or new game mechanics.

Use the revised dialogue samples to invite feedback on character individuality and the degree of exaggeration before a full production batch. Facial details are concept targets for later asset work, not an instruction to build models at this review stage.

## Relationships and writing guardrails

- Frankie and Vivian disagree about loyalty; later each recognizes that the other returned when they could have fled.
- Eve and Leon compare explanations and become complementary investigators. Neither exists to make the other look foolish.
- Eve recognizes Vivian's performed confidence; Vivian recognizes Eve's stage persona.
- Frankie remembers Leon as an employee; Leon insists on being treated as an equal.
- Each character needs quiet, practical, frightened, angry, and affectionate delivery as well as jokes.
- Accents support specific people. Differentiate cadence, vocabulary, breath, restraint, and emotional behavior before exaggerating regional markers.

## Visual production priority

Blender is the preferred asset workflow. Aim for original, grounded genre characters with readable faces and silhouettes at gameplay distances. Budget and browser performance take priority over close-up facial fidelity.

First pass: distinguishable bodies/clothing, shared rig where appropriate, movement and weapon animation, matching first-person sleeves/hands. Facial animation, lip sync, and fine skin detail are later polish. Establish actual mesh/texture budgets by profiling a four-player combat scene rather than inventing budgets now.
