# Vincent Voss

Working canon. Read the [cast bible](README.md) for chronology and implementation boundaries. Voss is an NPC; the player never needs to admire or forgive him to understand him.

## The boy who remembered names

Voss is approximately 58 in 1976. Working origin: Atlantic City, born around 1918, the son of a hotel porter and a woman who kept accounts for a boarding house. His parents worked hard and were often treated as part of the furniture by people they knew intimately: drinks, allergies, affairs, and the precise sound of a complaint beginning.

Vincent noticed that remembering someone's preference could open a door that honesty or effort would not. He learned to make a person feel recognized. He also noticed how quickly an impressive guest became helpless when a room key, a letter, or a discreet payment failed to arrive.

His parents were not murdered to explain his ambition. His father lived long enough to dislike how Vincent treated a junior employee. Voss remembers the criticism as evidence his father could not understand what success required. It is one of the few judgments he has never managed to purchase a revision to.

He worked coats, introductions, reservations, and increasingly private arrangements. At first he solved problems for powerful men. Then he began arranging circumstances so they needed him to solve the next problem. Violence entered as an available service. He learned to keep it out of the dining room, which is a matter of taste rather than mercy.

## Building something he genuinely loved

Voss built his Las Vegas business through useful friendships, criminal financing, excellent service, and a willingness to absorb immediate losses in exchange for future obligation. He wanted a place where arriving could feel like being chosen. The staff sometimes loved working an evening when his attention was generous. He really could make a room extraordinary.

His former wife, **Helena**, helped shape its interiors. She cared about circulation, comfortable seating, and what a space felt like to someone who was not buying the most expensive drink. He loved her certainty and enjoyed displaying her taste as evidence of his own.

They separated in 1969. She is alive. She left after understanding that every accommodation he made would eventually be entered as evidence in an argument about what she owed him. He has retained her best design decisions and describes them to visitors without always naming her.

He remembers what she liked to eat. He could arrange for it to appear anywhere in the city. The fact that this cannot make her want dinner with him is an offense he cannot quite articulate.

## The bargain

Working motive now selected: Voss sought to preserve the casino's prosperity and his control of it when its finances threatened to put him at the mercy of creditors. He knowingly accepted the collection of designated debtors as part of the arrangement. He presented the transactions to himself as adults agreeing to stakes; deception and the absence of a meaningful escape did not trouble that interpretation enough to stop him.

The original bargain's date, binding wording, precise selection rules, and why the current night requires four living signatures remain open. This dossier establishes his culpability and motive, not the legal mechanics of supernatural consent.

Voss expected to administer the bargain. He did not accept that he could become another asset the House would hold. That discovery makes his plan to transfer the obligation urgent. He can tell the crew true things and offer real help while intending to put them in his place.

Do not explain his cruelty solely as demonic possession or a tragic attempt to save a loved one. He made choices he found profitable. The House expanded their consequences; it did not invent his habits.

## What is genuine

He appreciates talent, remembers birthdays, dislikes a musician being heckled, and can be unexpectedly good company in a small room. He enjoys rehearsal more than he admits: watching someone labor to make an apparently effortless entrance fascinates him. He hums badly when there is nobody he needs to impress.

He loves inexpensive saltwater taffy and has it delivered in packaging more elegant than the contents deserve. Marlowe knows this and refuses to treat it as a scandal. Voss cheats at small private games even when the stake is trivial. Being caught annoys him less than someone refusing to play again.

He has paid real medical bills and remembered real grief. Generosity gives him pleasure before he turns it into leverage. The truth of the first feeling makes the second more dangerous.

He is capable of grief for a person he sacrificed. Feeling bad afterward does not change the fact that he would make the choice again if he believed control depended on it.

## Threat, vanity, and blind spots

In public, Voss can inflate any humiliation into an occasion. A broken window becomes evidence of a lively evening; a challenge becomes a joke that leaves the challenger alone in a laughing room. His theatricality is partly appetite and partly a practiced defense against ever looking cornered.

Private anger becomes small and precise. He names the person, reminds them of a debt, and gives a feasible instruction. He can wait. Do not rush every threatening line into a roar.

His blind spot is voluntary loyalty without an account attached. He assumes Frankie can be bought back, Eve can be flattered, Leon can be burdened, and Vivian can be offered a private exit. Some of those tactics should work temporarily. Their eventual refusal needs to cost them something.

He has survival skills and has personally hurt people; physical prowess is not his primary power. Do not turn him into a helpless bureaucrat or a flawless martial artist. His most consequential move is making someone else choose an action they will later call their own.

## Writing and reveal

He adjusts language to the listener. Public Voss makes the whole room his guest. With Leon he can be startlingly practical. With Frankie he recalls shared incidents without explaining them. With Vivian he performs the pleasure of meeting an equal while trying to establish ownership. With Marlowe, fragments of the ordinary man appear.

Examples:

> “Helena chose those lamps. I wanted something larger. She usually won the arguments worth remembering.”
>
> “I paid for your daughter's teeth, Frankie. Don't look at me as though we've only just met.”
>
> “You want me to say I was frightened. Fine. I was frightened. Look what I managed anyway.”

Use the family reference only after that relationship is established, not as a cheap first-line shock. His admission of fear should not excuse the people he selected for collection.

His final fate remains open. Whatever the ending, the crew's freedom should not depend on accepting his account of himself. A quiet exchange with Marlowe or a surviving design attribution can expose more humanity than an explanatory villain monologue.
