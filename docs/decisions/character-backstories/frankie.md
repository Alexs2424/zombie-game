# Frankie “Chips” Caruso

Working canon. Read the [cast bible](README.md) for chronology and implementation boundaries.

## The man before the suit

Frankie is 47 in 1976, raised in Newark, the son of a delivery driver and a school cafeteria worker. His childhood was crowded, noisy, and ordinary. There was always someone arguing about a bill. He learned that the easiest way to end an argument was to become useful: carry the refrigerator, collect the overdue payment, stand beside the smaller kid when the older boys came back.

He was not a brilliant boxer. He was good at staying upright after a better boxer expected him to fall. A succession of loading jobs, club doors, and cash collections taught him to recognize the pause before somebody reached for a weapon. He liked being the person whose arrival made a room settle down. He liked it more than he admitted.

He followed a club manager west in the late 1950s. Voss hired him after watching him remove an armed guest without overturning a table. Frankie remembers being praised for restraint. The guest remembers the broken wrist. Both accounts are true.

“Chips” began as a staff joke. Early in his casino employment, Frankie spotted counterfeit chips and returned them to their owner one at a time until the man explained where they came from. He would prefer people assume the nickname means he is lucky at cards. He is not.

## What Voss bought

Voss gave Frankie a respectable title, better suits, and dependable money. When Frankie's daughter needed dental work, the payment arrived before he asked. Frankie understood this as friendship. Voss understood it as making an employee harder to lose.

Frankie developed rules to separate his work from the uglier men doing similar work: never frighten a child to reach a parent, never bring business into a family kitchen, never take a man's wedding ring. The rules did not prevent him from breaking a bookkeeper's hand. They made him feel better afterward because he had left the ring alone.

In 1973 he escorted a guest named **Martin Keene** behind the secure cashier barrier. Keene asked him to wait. Frankie obeyed the instruction to leave instead. When the man did not emerge, Frankie accepted an explanation involving a private car and unpaid debts. He did not believe it enough to repeat it to himself clearly.

He left Voss's employment in 1974 after months of unreliable payments and increasingly strange work. He tells people he walked out on principle. He stayed until there was a financial reason to leave. His accumulated pay remains outstanding, and closing night is supposedly his last chance to collect it.

Keene's final fate and relationship to the wider mystery remain open. He is not Elias Varga, and the name should not appear in existing hotel records without an explicit quest revision.

## A life outside this building

Frankie's former wife, **Ruth**, is alive and runs the office of an appliance-repair business. Their daughter **Jo**, 19, is learning projection work at a cinema. Jo can thread a projector, has opinions about terrible dubbing, and dislikes men explaining her equipment to her.

Frankie remembers every installment of child support and misses conversations because he does not know what to say once money is accounted for. He secretly paid a repair bill for Jo's car. She wanted him to ask before going behind her back. He remains baffled that something generous could also be controlling.

He keeps a cheap postcard from her inside an expensive wallet. Its message is about a broken vending machine, not love or forgiveness. He rereads it anyway. His watch stopped years ago. He checks it when embarrassed and will admit it is broken only if someone notices first.

He likes elaborate radio competitions, terrible monster pictures, and keeping his shoes clean. He falls asleep before the end of most films. He is good at wrapping gifts and buys the wrong gifts with remarkable consistency.

## Personality under pressure

Frankie sees fear as something you manage with posture, repetition, and keeping your hands occupied. He gives orders because uncertainty embarrasses him. A quiet, specific correction works better on him than a public challenge. His apologies usually begin with an explanation of why the other person should not have made him worry.

He can be extravagant about small disrespect and almost silent about a serious threat. Someone ruining his jacket may get a speech. Someone threatening Jo gets his full attention and very few words. Physical pain makes him angrier at witnesses than at the wound.

He is affectionate, vain, impatient, superstitious about completely ordinary objects, and much easier to flatter than he believes. He will share ammunition without counting it, then spend five minutes arguing over who took his lighter.

## Competence, limits, and moral pressure

He knows doorways, body language, and how to commit to close violence without freezing. He is useful in a crisis because he makes a decision and physically follows through. He is not an expert investigator or a military tactician. An unfamiliar mechanism makes him impatient; an apparently respectful authority can still manipulate him.

He refuses to use a helpless person as bait. His harder test is accepting that protection does not entitle him to obedience. Vivian can reject his advice and still deserve his help. Leon can condemn his old work without becoming an enemy.

His map arc should cost him Voss's approval and his own preferred account of his life. Recovering the money would let him call the relationship finished on business terms. Helping expose Keene's disappearance means admitting it was never merely business. Escape does not automatically earn Jo's admiration or a victim's forgiveness.

## Writing and delivery

Use names, remembered details, concrete threats, and reluctant practical care. Let him revise himself when he gets too close to affection. His North Jersey accent supplies rhythm; do not spell every dropped consonant or substitute mob quotations for personality.

Examples of ordinary speech, not production-triggered barks:

> “Jo's working the projector now. Whole theater goes dark without her. That's a real job.”
>
> “Don't thank me. Just—next time you move, tell somebody.”
>
> “I left him his ring. Used to think that counted for something.”

The last line belongs after Keene's history is revealed, in a quiet window. It is an admission of self-deception, not proof that Keene wore a ring unless that detail is explicitly authored into the incident.

Reveal his family through one conversational detail before revealing the crime. The contrast makes his guilt human without redeeming him automatically. A postcard, an old employee card, and the useless watch are optional evidence props, not required collectibles or new mechanics.
