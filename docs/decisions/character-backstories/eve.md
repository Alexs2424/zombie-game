# Evelyn “Eve” Vale

Working canon. Read the [cast bible](README.md) for chronology and implementation boundaries.

## Learning to own a room

Eve is 34, Black British, raised in south London. Her mother **June** altered costumes and ordinary clothes at the same kitchen table. Her father drove a bus and distrusted anyone who took longer than a minute to get to the point. Eve grew up around people who could appreciate a beautiful entrance and still ask who had sewn the lining.

She learned that a confident introduction could buy the seconds needed to prove she belonged on a stage. Touring small venues taught her to keep going when an audience talked over her, a cue failed, or a manager decided she would be more marketable in someone else's act. The polished stage voice is a skill she built. At home, when irritated or laughing helplessly, it loosens.

At 22 she met **Arthur “Art” Bell**, a stage carpenter seven years her senior, while trying to make an escape cabinet fit through a dressing-room door. He shortened the cabinet without asking permission. She accused him of ruining the trick. He invited her to explain which part of the trick depended on getting stuck in a corridor.

They became a double act: Eve could direct attention; Art could build something worth directing it away from. They were friends and professional partners, not lovers. Their intimacy was practical. He knew when her wrist hurt. She knew when his joke meant a mechanism was not safe yet.

## The compromise she enjoyed

Voss offered a residency in 1972: regular money, proper rehearsal time, and her name where people outside the building could see it. Eve accepted. Art warned her that permanent employment meant somebody else owned the time between performances. She thought he was afraid of success.

The first posters named both of them. Later publicity made Art smaller, then omitted him. Eve objected once, accepted an explanation about space, and enjoyed the larger lettering. She began presenting shared discoveries as the natural products of her brilliance. She always intended to correct the record when their position was secure enough.

Three weeks before closing night, Art found a discrepancy between the stage-service plans and a route he had measured. Eve dismissed his concern during a vicious argument about credit. He called the casino another box she was pretending to have escaped. Her last remark to him was a polished, unforgivable little insult about being replaceable.

He disappeared after continuing his investigation. Voss says arrangements were made to forward his effects. Eve has come to retrieve them personally. She intends to learn where he went, but she has not yet admitted that she might be arriving too late.

Art's fate and the exact route he investigated remain open. This history does not create a currently traversable stage corridor or require building an entire theater. A schematic, work order or recorded observation can establish the discrepancy later.

## What she is like when nobody is watching

She still hems her own trousers and becomes territorial if someone offers to do it faster. She enjoys dreadful jokes when they are delivered by someone with no ambition to impress her. She loses at dominoes to June and invents elaborate objections to the scoring. Her family has not been killed or estranged to make her interesting.

She likes spicy mustard, bad weather viewed from indoors, and the moment after a train leaves a station. She hates wet cuffs, casual lateness, and being asked to do a trick while eating. She keeps Art's blunt little pencil because it fits behind her ear; she is not ready to call it a keepsake.

Eve can be absurdly generous with equipment and ungenerous with credit. She will spend an hour helping a nervous beginner, then sulk because a review praises the lighting more than her. Her vanity is funny because she has done the work. Her cruelty hurts because she knows exactly where to put it.

## Danger and vulnerability

Touring taught her escape knots, balance, timing, and keeping a working blade where a costume does not show it. She practices difficult motions until she can execute them while frightened. That translates into decisive physical action, not supernatural perception or mastery of every weapon.

She is most dangerous when an opponent wants to be admired. She can make them look at her while somebody else moves. She is weakest when the problem threatens her self-image as the cleverest observer. She may defend an elegant explanation after Leon has found a plain fact that disproves it.

She refuses to abandon someone inside a mechanism she told them was safe. The deeper test is letting another person take charge and credit. Her progress should be audible: early Eve announces what she has deduced; later Eve says who helped her see it. She does not need to become humble or stop enjoying applause.

If Art is alive, he is entitled to anger and may not want their partnership back. If he is dead, discovering that does not settle what she owes his work. Do not make forgiveness an automatic quest reward.

## Writing and delivery

Public Eve gives a thought shape before speaking: a premise, a pause, a decisive ending. Private Eve can abandon a sentence. Real fear shrinks her vocabulary. Her elaborate humor should coexist with plain fatigue and specific care.

Examples of conversational range:

> “Mother still calls it showing off. Thirty-four years. Consistent reviews.”
>
> “Art built that catch. See the extra screw? He never trusted anything just because it looked finished.”
>
> “I knew he'd be hurt. That's why I said it.”

The catch line requires an actual identified piece of Art's equipment. The confession should follow evidence of their argument, not appear as random combat chatter.

A revised poster, repaired cuff, and pencil can tell more than a speech about ambition. Any prop is optional future dressing. Do not use an inaccessible guest room as its assumed location.
