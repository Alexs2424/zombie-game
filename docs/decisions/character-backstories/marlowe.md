# Edwin Marlowe

Working canon. Read the [cast bible](README.md) for chronology and implementation boundaries. Everyone normally calls him Marlowe. First name Edwin is a private human detail, not a password or puzzle answer by default.

## A life behind counters

Marlowe appears about 63. His human history begins near Leeds, where his family kept a modest public house. He grew up learning that silence could be attentive, that a regular's order changed with their circumstances, and that an adult could ruin a week while looking perfectly respectable for an evening.

He worked in hotels and shipboard bars before reaching Las Vegas. He describes these years in practical details: an impossible staircase, a broken ice machine, a piano player who would never finish a song. His exact birth year and supernatural aging remain open; do not manufacture a timeline contradiction merely to signal mystery.

He was working for Voss by 1959. He remembers an owner who carried chairs when the room was short of staff and practiced introductions when he thought nobody could hear. He also remembers the first time a guest vanished and people were instructed to treat it as an administrative inconvenience. Those memories have coexisted for too long.

## Why he stayed

In the ordinary business, Marlowe served as an informal keeper of emergencies. He held keys for staff, found a quiet chair for a frightened guest, and advanced small amounts of money without reporting it to management. He believed this made the building safer with him in it.

When departures became suspicious, he continued managing the practical aftermath. He accepted luggage, redirected questions, and let people believe that the official record might be enough. He did not choose the victims, but his competence made their disappearance less disruptive.

He kept private notes about discrepancies. For a long time he called the notes evidence he would use when it mattered. They also let him feel that recording the wrong was a kind of resistance even while he continued smoothing its consequences.

Working decision: no blanket magical rule prevents Marlowe from telling the truth. His initial evasions come from fear, incomplete knowledge, complicity, and uncertainty over whom he can trust. If later quest design requires a specific supernatural restriction, it must be stated and reconciled here rather than used retroactively to excuse every silence.

His exact obligation to the House, whether it prevents his physical departure, and what protects him during ordinary gameplay remain open. His old human reasons for staying can be established before any supernatural answer.

## Someone still uses his first name

His younger sister **Elsie** runs a small pub in England and has repeatedly offered him a room upstairs. She writes about plumbing, customers, and the garden before asking whether he has decided to come home. She does not know the casino's supernatural truth.

He writes replies in beautiful, ordinary detail and postpones giving an arrival date. He keeps an unused luggage label in his drawer. It has been ready for longer than he cares to calculate.

Elsie is not an innocent he must rescue or a ghost used to punish him. She is a living person whose invitation makes his choices harder to explain. A future return would require telling someone who loves him what he allowed to happen.

He enjoys crossword puzzles, has a poor singing voice he uses without embarrassment when alone, and follows a local amateur football club through mailed reports that arrive late. He has strong opinions about an unremarkable biscuit. He cannot bring himself to throw away a functional teaspoon.

## The courtesy and the cruelty

Marlowe's absurd commitment to service can make him seem invulnerable. It is a ritual he uses to make one small part of the room predictable. He will put a coaster beneath a bloody glass because he can still decide where the glass belongs.

He can be deeply kind and uncomfortably judgmental. He remembers when someone failed a friend and occasionally lets the knowledge sharpen a reply that did not need sharpening. His silence can punish. He knows how to make a powerful person feel that an entire room has noticed their poor manners without saying anything untrue.

He dislikes waste, needless humiliation, and people who confuse loudness with authority. He also dislikes admitting that he enjoys being the person everyone comes to. Like Leon, he can mistake indispensability for a moral reason to remain.

He can handle a weapon and has ended fights in his bar. Do not give him a surprise history as an unbeatable assassin. His danger is patience, memory, and a willingness to say an exact thing at the moment somebody most needs it concealed.

## The relationship with Voss

They have spent years at the same late-night counter. Voss knows how Marlowe takes tea. Marlowe knows which of the owner's grand anecdotes began as a small embarrassment. They sometimes enjoy each other's company without bargaining for anything, which makes their usual arrangement more tragic.

Voss permits corrections from Marlowe that he would punish from another employee. He treats that permission as proof of friendship. Marlowe has sometimes accepted it as enough.

His map arc is choosing a specific act of help that compromises his position. Giving the crew evidence, contradicting Voss publicly, or surrendering a practical protection are possible forms; the exact quest action is open. A cryptic hint delivered from safety is not the culmination of his moral story.

He must not explain every mystery. He knows what crossed the counter, what people said within earshot, and what he recorded or was told. The crew can discover something he did not know, and he can be honestly frightened by it.

## Writing and delivery

His northern English voice is plain and patient. A small change in pace can expose anger. Dry humor usually addresses a fact the other person wanted ignored. Sometimes he offers direct comfort without an elegant sting at the end.

Examples:

> “My sister's got a room for me. Says the roof leaks. She's always been better at invitations.”
>
> “No, Edwin. My mother liked it. We needn't make an evening of it.”
>
> “I wrote their names down. Thought that made me different from the men who forgot them.”

The name answer requires another speaker asking his first name; do not have him volunteer it as a portent. The final line follows disclosure of his notes and should leave room for the crew's discomfort rather than prompt automatic forgiveness.

His ordinary letters, private discrepancy notes, and luggage label are possible future props. They are distinct from the existing hotel collection register and must not silently replace its authored contents.
