# Vivian Cross

Working canon. Read the [cast bible](README.md) for chronology and implementation boundaries.

## A talent she chose to misuse

Vivian is 29, Mexican-American, raised in Los Angeles. Her birth surname is **Cruz**. She adopted Cross professionally because it fit the person she wanted strangers to imagine, and because she liked choosing an answer before anyone asked the question. The name change is ordinary self-invention, not a supernatural identity twist.

Her mother **Estela** worked a cinema box office. Her stepfather **Luis** repaired upholstery. They were neither saints nor secret criminals. Vivian watched people change their voices when asking for something and discovered she could do it better than most adults around her.

She could have made a respectable living selling nearly anything. Respectability offered less applause and required a longer wait. She enjoyed taking money from someone who believed they were about to take hers. The fact that a mark was unpleasant became a useful excuse, then a habit of deciding everyone with money must deserve the lesson.

She learned cards, social entry, forged introductions, and how to leave a room while the host was still explaining why she could not. Violence frightens her. She has learned to act through it, but the aftermath sometimes catches up with her in shaking hands she hides by adjusting a glove.

## The job she cannot explain away

Her older brother **Tomas** ran a small print shop. He liked her stories and knew enough to ask which parts involved him before laughing. She used his business details to give a fraudulent deal legitimacy without telling him the whole plan. When the deal collapsed, the consequences reached his customers and credit before she could disappear them with a clever fix.

Tomas lost the shop. He is alive, employed elsewhere, and angry for a reason that will not go away when she makes him smile. Vivian sends money through increasingly inventive channels because he returns anything addressed in her usual name. Some reaches him. None has produced the conversation she wants.

Her account of the incident originally made him naive and her unlucky. She now knows the accurate version and dislikes how few flattering words it contains. This is the wound beneath her fixation on the vault: one spectacular score could let her pay him back without asking how to apologize.

She has a moral boundary about using children or a person's dependents as leverage. The business failure shows that she can violate the spirit of her own rule while congratulating herself on observing its literal wording. Do not treat her code as proof she only hurts bad people.

## Why this casino

An invitation bearing the name **Celia March** came within her reach. Vivian obtained it, assembled enough of an identity to attend, and found reasons to believe the vault would be vulnerable during closing. She thinks the opportunity is the result of her own research and nerve.

The dossier's working secret is that Voss's side deliberately placed the opportunity in her path. She does not know this at entry. Evidence later reveals the historical Celia March is dead. Who March was, when she died, how she relates to the original bargain, and the exact way the invitation was planted remain open.

Vivian genuinely wants the money. She is not secretly an investigator with a noble mission. As the night continues, she discovers that having people who depend on her is inconveniently different from having people she can fool.

## A person between cons

She loves terrible romantic melodramas and objects fiercely when someone talks over the last scene. She keeps restaurant matchbooks from places where something went well. She can remember a stranger's habits after one conversation and forget an appointment she does not want to attend.

She has strong, usually wrong opinions about horses. She tips extravagantly after a successful score and resents small mandatory charges. She is scrupulous about paying a tailor because she understands exactly what that work costs. She can still convince herself that a bank clerk's extra hours cleaning up her scheme are somebody else's problem.

She likes being liked. She prefers to describe it as maintaining options. She becomes jealous when someone else is charming without seeming to try. Given an unearned compliment, she knows it is bait and still wants the next one.

## Competence, panic, and attachment

Vivian identifies what someone wants and where their confidence makes them careless. She can bluff long enough for the crew to act, spot false paperwork, and remember a detail another person dismissed. She is physically quick and accustomed to an ugly exit. She is not an effortless safecracker, a master engineer, or a person who can talk a mindless zombie into a bargain.

Her speech speeds up when a plan begins to fail. When she is actually terrified, it stops being clever. A brief “Please. Look at me” can expose more than a monologue about her past.

She initially assumes Frankie wants obedience, Eve wants admiration, and Leon wants a job to solve. She is partly right and dangerously incomplete. The moment each helps her without collecting anything unsettles her more than a demand would.

Her arc is returning when the best personal option is to leave. That action should cost her something tangible or place her in real danger. Do not award moral redemption merely because she jokes about becoming nicer. Tomas still gets to decide whether he wants a relationship with her afterward.

## Writing and delivery

Vivian's Los Angeles accent stays natural. Ethnicity does not require accented English. She borrows the formality of whoever she is persuading and abandons it when speaking honestly. Use quick agreements, flattering questions, sudden corrections, and shameless attempts to regain status after embarrassment.

Examples:

> “Cross fits on a business card. Cruz fits on my mother's envelopes. You can use either if you're bringing money.”
>
> “My brother had a print shop. I helped. ... That's the short version. It's also the dishonest one.”
>
> “Don't say you're proud of me. I haven't worked out what to charge for that.”

The last line should be an awkward defense after earned affection, not a catchphrase for every compliment. Her most intimate line may be a simple acceptance without a price attached.

An unpaid invoice or returned envelope can hint at Tomas before she speaks about him. Do not put his full history into a collectible paragraph before the character has had a chance to be funny, resourceful, and difficult.
