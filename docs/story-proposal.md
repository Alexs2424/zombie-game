# Last Jackpot — Story Proposal

Status: historical initial pitch, retained for context. The current source of truth is [the narrative decisions folder](decisions/README.md). Where this pitch differs from those documents, the decisions folder takes precedence. This document describes a narrative direction, not implemented gameplay.

## Core direction

A casino where **“the house always wins” is a literal supernatural rule.**

The story should evoke the mystery, character chemistry, environmental storytelling, and layered Easter egg quests of classic Call of Duty Zombies while developing an original setting and mythology. The surface premise is easy to understand: survive the casino. Underneath it is a stranger story that players can uncover together.

The existing casino floor, Marlowe’s bar, High Roller Club, staff passages, and hotel provide the foundation. Familiar spaces gradually reveal a hidden purpose as the investigation progresses.

### Opening hook

> On closing night, four strangers arrive at a dying Las Vegas casino. At midnight, every slot machine pays out, the exits disappear, and the dead return to collect. The owner promises a way out. First, they have to settle a debt.

The recommended direction is **The House Always Wins**, with some of the buried history and haunting atmosphere of **Closing Night** below. Give the supernatural rules one clear origin; let the deeper history become complicated later.

## Alternate story directions

| Direction | Core premise | Casino boss’s role | Tone |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **The House Always Wins** | The owner made a bargain to keep his failing casino alive. Nobody who owes the house can leave—even after death. | A charming owner trapped inside his own bargain. He needs the crew’s help, but his escape may come at their expense. | Occult mystery, crime, dark humor |
| **The Last Jackpot** | A machine beneath the casino manipulates probability. Somebody won something that should have been impossible, and reality broke. | He financed the machine and wants it restored before the crew discovers what it consumes. | Retro science fiction, strange experiments, escalating unreality |
| **Closing Night** | Years ago, the owner killed a room full of guests to bury a secret. The casino now replays that night, with four new people cast in the missing roles. | Host, murderer, and possibly another prisoner of the replay. | Ghost story, conspiracy, glamorous decay |

These are alternatives to develop or borrow from, not three simultaneous explanations for the outbreak.

## The four playable characters

The protagonists belong in the casino setting, have different reasons to be there, and each have a reason to distrust its owner. Names are working names.

### Frankie “Chips” Caruso — Former casino enforcer

Frankie returned to collect money the boss owes him. He knows the service corridors and where the bodies went. He acts like the crew’s tough older brother, but recognizes some of the zombies.

> “I threw that guy out in ’68. Apparently it didn’t take.”

His loyalty to the owner’s old reputation puts him at odds with the evidence the crew uncovers.

### Evelyn “Eve” Vale — Headlining magician

Eve came to retrieve something the owner locked away. She understands misdirection and keeps noticing that the casino’s architecture works like a stage illusion. Her missing partner may have discovered the secret first.

> “That door’s a trick. What’s behind it probably isn’t.”

She knows when the crew is being shown something designed to distract them from the real question.

### Leon Mercer — Casino maintenance engineer

Leon came to shut the building down. He knows its electrical system, service lifts, and machinery that never appeared on the plans. He previously helped install something beneath the casino without understanding its purpose.

> “There are six floors on the plans. So why does this elevator have a seven?”

He tries to explain what is happening mechanically, even as the building stops obeying the rules he knows.

### Vivian Cross — Professional cheat

Vivian came to rob the vault during closing night. She can spot rigged games and forged records. Her invitation was issued in the name of someone who died decades ago.

> “I came here to rob a casino. This is a considerable change in terms.”

She is quick to question the owner’s instructions and has no patience for Frankie’s nostalgia.

### Crew chemistry and co-op design

Their chemistry matters more than elaborate biographies. Frankie defends the boss’s old reputation; Vivian mocks him for it. Leon tries to explain everything mechanically; Eve knows when an explanation is becoming an excuse.

Initially, give all four shared gameplay capabilities. Their identities should come through dialogue, reactions, and personal clues. A puzzle should not become impossible because one player disconnected or nobody picked Leon.

The main story should bring these strangers together into a recurring crew that can carry future maps.

## The casino boss

Working name: **Vincent Voss**.

The initial recommendation is to make Voss an NPC rather than one of the four playable characters. Four protagonists and a fifth character manipulating them leave room for competing interpretations of his motives.

Voss is always immaculately dressed and speaks as though the catastrophe is a temporary inconvenience. His first contact comes through the PA:

> “Ladies and gentlemen, a minor interruption. Please disregard anyone attempting to eat the other guests.”

He guides the crew through restoring parts of the casino. Each success makes him more helpful—and lets him control more of the building.

The central distinction is that **Voss owns the casino, but something else is the House**. He made a deal with it. He cannot simply order the outbreak to stop.

The desired response to Voss is reluctant affection. He is funny, useful, and occasionally brave, which makes discovering what he has done more interesting. Give him one sincere attachment: perhaps he really does care about Marlowe, who stayed when every other employee left.

His final characterization remains open: smooth mob owner, eccentric showman, or intimidating kingpin.

## Marlowe’s role

Marlowe is the quieter counterweight to Voss. Voss offers explanations; Marlowe offers unsettling observations.

> “Funny. He told the last four that, too.”

Marlowe’s familiarity with the casino’s history makes him a source of optional clues. How much he knows, why he stayed, and what he owes the House remain questions to develop.

## Main Easter egg: settle the debt

The main quest is an optional investigation layered over survival. Each step answers one question and creates a worse one. Its clues should be discoverable in the environment, and the finale should pay off what the crew learned.

### 1. Find evidence of an earlier crew

A photograph behind the bar shows four guests holding the same invitations as the players. One invitation has a handwritten warning:

> “Don’t open the cage.”

This establishes that the current crew may be repeating something that happened before.

### 2. Restore the surveillance system

Voss says the cameras will locate an exit. Instead, the monitors show recordings from closing night, including footage of someone entering a room that has no door on the current map.

The crew now has a concrete mystery to investigate, and its first reason to doubt Voss’s explanation.

### 3. Reconstruct a rigged hand in the High Roller Club

Security footage, a dealer’s notes, and marked cards reveal the final hand played in the club. Recreating it opens the missing room.

This should be a deduction, not a requirement to wait for a random winning hand.

### 4. Recover the original debt ledger

The hotel contains evidence of what guests actually wagered: years of their lives, memories, and someone else’s future.

The zombies are people whose debts never closed.

### 5. Discover why these four were invited

Each protagonist is connected to the original bargain. Their invitations were chosen deliberately.

Voss needs four living signatures to transfer ownership of the casino—and its debt.

The exact personal connections are still to be developed. They should give each character a meaningful revelation without requiring a particular character to be present to finish the quest.

### 6. Prepare a fixed game against the House

The crew uses what it has learned to rig one final wager. Players coordinate surveillance, power, the dealer’s table, and the vault mechanism while defending themselves across the casino.

Allow flexible roles and a smaller-team fallback. The sequence should reward coordination without depending on four simultaneous character-specific interactions.

### 7. Face the Collector

The final enemy is the House’s debt collector. The mechanisms prepared during the quest create opportunities to damage it, so the finale pays off the investigation.

The Collector’s appearance, combat mechanics, and relationship to Voss remain open for development.

## Proposed ending and wider story

The crew wins its freedom and leaves together. Then Vivian opens the bag she stole from the vault.

Inside is a deed to another property.

This gives the first map a satisfying conclusion and a reason for the same four characters to return. The wider story can explore the House’s reach through other properties without requiring the first map to explain the entire mythology.

Voss’s ultimate fate remains undecided.

## Smaller Easter eggs

These should vary in purpose: rewards, jokes, character moments, and unsettling clues. Not every odd object needs to belong to the main quest.

- **Marlowe’s usual:** Find an old cocktail recipe and order it. His cheerful bartender routine drops, and he tells the crew one honest thing about the owner.
- **The fifth reflection:** A hotel mirror briefly shows someone standing with the four players. Later, the crew finds that person’s portrait.
- **Employee of the month:** Staff photographs reveal that the same people worked here across impossible spans of time.
- **An encore:** Restore the lounge performance equipment to unlock a song and a ghostly final performance.
- **A room that remembers:** An optional hotel room contains a personal object for each protagonist. Their dialogue reveals something they have been hiding from the others.

## Questions for the next story pass

- Is Voss primarily a smooth mob owner, an eccentric showman, or an intimidating kingpin?
- What exactly did he ask the House for, and what did he knowingly sacrifice?
- How is each protagonist connected to the original bargain?
- Who were the earlier four guests, and what happened when they followed Voss’s instructions?
- What does Marlowe know, and why has he stayed?
- What happens to Voss when the crew wins?
- Which rules of the House should be understood by the end of this map, and which mysteries should carry forward?
